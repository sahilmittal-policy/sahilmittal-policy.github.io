The problem

Greater Boston’s bus network carries the region’s most transit-dependent riders on some of its slowest and least reliable services. Black bus riders in Boston spend roughly 64 more hours a year in transit than white riders.

Over a weekend at the MIT Policy Hackathon, my teammates Anushri Gade and Rahul Tejannavar and I asked three questions: which routes does that penalty fall hardest on, can it be fixed without a capital megaproject, and what would fixing it cost?

How we found the corridors

Ranking routes by need alone rewards lightly-used shuttles through very poor neighbourhoods. Ranking by ridership alone rewards busy routes through affluent ones. We needed something that asked a blunter question: where are the most equity-weighted riders?

So we built a Transit Dependence Index for every route in the network — population-weighted shares of zero-vehicle households, residents below 200% of the poverty line, non-white residents, and transit commuters, measured within an 800 metre walkshed of every stop and normalised against the system average. Then we multiplied it by ridership.

Three routes — 28, 23 and 66 — came out first, second and third in the entire MBTA bus network. Then we checked what service those riders actually get.

All three run longer than the median peak runtime. Route 66 takes nearly 49 minutes at peak with the worst on-time performance in the group.

The more you depend on the bus, the more you are punished by slow and unreliable service.

Why it’s a labour-market problem

Using LEHD LODES employment microdata, we found the corridor is itself a major job centre — over 69,000 health and social assistance jobs sit inside it — and yet only about one quarter of the jobs held by corridor residents are located there. Residents work at more than 12,000 distinct workplace blocks.

Most riders are passing through to somewhere else, usually via a rail transfer. That reframes a local transit intervention as a regional access problem, and it is the finding the recommendation rests on.

What it costs, and what it returns

Not a megaproject. Dedicated lanes on congested segments, transit signal priority, queue jumps at bottlenecks, far-side stops and stop consolidation — tools Boston already deploys, at roughly $0.5M a mile. Ten miles of treatment, $6.5 million all in.

The model keeps three benefit buckets separate, because they are not the same kind of money:

Bucket Per year In the payback?
Net operating savings, after reinvesting half into service $3.16M ✅
New rail fare revenue from better bus–rail feeding $0.56M ✅
Rider time value (1.23M hours at $10–20/hr) $12.3–24.5M ❌

Rider time is a societal benefit, not agency cash. Mixing the two into a payback figure is how cost-benefit analyses get dismissed by the people who have to sign them off. So payback and NPV use the first two buckets only — and it still clears in 1.7 years.

Does it survive pessimism?

The interesting question isn’t what the model says at the central case. It’s how far you have to push the assumptions before the recommendation breaks.

The package pays back inside five years even at a 5% runtime reduction — half the bottom of the range documented for comparable bus-priority projects.

A note on what came after

The original analysis notebooks were lost after the event. What survived was the memo, the deck, and a working document with the intermediate tables and part of the original GeoPandas code.

Rather than let the work disappear, I rebuilt it as a documented, tested Python package. The cost-benefit model is verified — it reproduces every published figure, and 42 tests pin it there so a refactor can’t quietly change the answer. The spatial pipeline is reconstructed but not re-executed, and the repository says so plainly.

It also documents three things that don’t reconcile, including a composite index whose published formula doesn’t reproduce its published value. That index is implemented as published, and the repository reports what it yields rather than back-fitting to the reported number.
